Bayou & Blazer Playbook

How a working AI stylist for Houston men becomes a business: revenue, growth, store partnerships, legal footing, and the expansion map — womenswear and new cities included.

Where you stand

You already own the hard part

Most people pitching an idea like this have a deck. You have a working product: an eight-expert AI pipeline that turns one conversation into a priced, dated, store-by-store wardrobe plan in about a minute — with a 43-store directory that re-verifies itself monthly by live web research, a stylist persona customers talk to like a person, plan chat, outfit matrices, purchase tracking, and cross-device claim codes. Monthly infrastructure cost today: roughly $7–25 in hosting and API credits.

Everything below builds on that asset.

Revenue

Growth

Getting popular in Houston

  1. Make your wife the face everywhere. The Kyla brand is already hers. Short vertical videos: her voiceover reading a real plan's best lines, before/after fits, "what Kyla told a lawyer to say at the tailor." Instagram + TikTok, Houston hashtags. This is your only marketing job for month one.
  2. Own the search. "Personal stylist Houston," "what to wear to the rodeo," "CERAWeek what to wear," "Houston tailor recommendations." Marisol's almanac answers already exist — publish them as pages on a simple marketing site. Nobody else is writing this for Houston with real store names.
  3. Time the calendar you already track. The app knows CERAWeek, OTC, rodeo season, and gala season. Run pushes three to six weeks before each — exactly when the app tells its own users to shop.
  4. Referrals through claim codes. The code system doubles as a referral hook: "Send a friend — when they finish a plan, you both get a month of Kyla+."
  5. Reddit and group chats. r/houston, r/malefashionadvice, office Slack channels. One genuinely great free plan screenshot does more than an ad.

Partnerships

Getting the stores involved

Start with independents — Hamilton Shirts, QC Tailors, Q Clothier, Rye 51 — where you can talk to an owner, not a regional manager. The ladder:

  1. They're already listed. Walk in with the app open: "We've been sending customers here with a script for your counter. Wanted you to know."
  2. Prove the traffic. Have customers say "Kyla sent me" (put it in the plan's logistics lines). After a month: "We sent you N fitted customers who each spent about $X."
  3. The referral deal. A flat fee or small percentage per customer who mentions the app. Handshake first; paper later. The tracked claim code shown at the counter is the receipt.
  4. The paid tier. Once three or four stores pay referrals, offer featured placement and verified profiles — the store's own words in its listing, their trunk-show dates pushed to users.

What you never sell: the plan's picks. The moment placement buys a recommendation, Kyla stops being trustworthy and the product dies. Featured slots live in the directory, never inside a customer's plan.

Legal & IP

Copyright, trademark, and one real risk

  • Copyright: your code and content are automatically copyrighted the moment they're written — registration is optional evidence. Registering with the U.S. Copyright Office (~$65) is cheap and worth doing once the product settles. It is not the urgent item.
  • Trademark is the urgent item: the name "Bayou & Blazer" and Kyla's identity are the brand. A USPTO application runs roughly $250–350 per class. Do this before marketing spend, not after.
  • Form an LLC before revenue (Texas filing ~$300), add plain-language Terms of Service and a privacy policy (you handle photos — say what happens to them), and put the app's "style advice, not guarantees" nature in writing.
  • Your wife's likeness: fine with her consent — get a one-page written release between her and the LLC anyway. Clean paper now prevents mess later, especially if you ever sell.

Resolved

The athlete-homage names were a real legal exposure, and they are gone. The seven expert personas were originally named for living Houston sports figures. Texas right-of-publicity law is strong, and a fan-homage disclaimer does not license commercial use of a celebrity's identity to sell a product; as a free project it likely drew no attention, but as a revenue business it was the kind of thing that draws a demand letter. The roster is now Theo, Marisol, Vinh, Simone, Ade, Priya, and Elena: original characters with their own Houston backstories, carrying no third-party identity. Kyla and the app name were always yours and are unaffected.

Valuation

What the app is worth

The honest answer: today, as a pre-revenue product with no users, its market value is close to zero — buyers pay for traction, not code. That is not bad news; it's the roadmap. Small software businesses commonly sell for three to five times annual revenue. The same product at $3,000/month of revenue is a ~$100–180K asset; at $10K/month it's several hundred thousand dollars — and the city playbook below multiplies that.

What you own right now that does have value: a finished product that would cost real money to rebuild, a self-refreshing data moat, a brand with a face, and a repeatable formula. Value it by what it lets you do next, not what someone would pay today.

Expansion

Women's line, then the city playbook

Womenswear is the natural second product, and Kyla is even more at home there. The engine doesn't change — what changes is data and voice: a women's store directory, dress-code decoders, fit-and-body playbook, staples showcase, and interview adjustments. Realistically a few weeks of focused work on top of what exists. Run it inside the same app ("Who are we dressing today?") to share the brand and the store relationships.

Cities are packs, not new apps. Everything Houston-specific already lives in data files: the store directory, the events calendar, shopping districts, industry decoders, the monthly refresh. A "Dallas pack" is those files researched for Dallas — the personas, pipeline, and app carry over. Keep one codebase and one brand with a city selector; separate apps per city would multiply your maintenance by the number of cities. Sequence: Houston womenswear → Dallas → Austin — and only after Houston has paying users proving the model.
